# Why Reinforcement Learning Exists — What Random Search on CartPole Taught Me

**Context:** I wanted to learn RL by tinkering rather than by reading, so I started with CartPole — the "hello world" of the field. A cart slides on a track, a pole is hinged on top of it, and you push left or right to keep the pole from falling. I wrote a controller by hand, felt clever, and then tried the dumbest possible alternative: guessing. The guessing won. That result is the reason this entry exists, because the honest question it raises is *if guessing solves this, why does an entire field exist?*

---

## 1. The environment, and the four numbers

CartPole hands you an observation of four numbers at every step:

| symbol | meaning |
|---|---|
| `x` | cart position |
| `ẋ` | cart velocity |
| `θ` | pole angle |
| `θ̇` | pole angular velocity |

You return one of two actions — push left or push right. You get `+1` reward for every timestep the pole stays up, and the episode ends at 500 steps. So a perfect score is 500, and the score *is* the number of steps you survived.

Underneath, the environment is not mysterious. It's Euler integration of a four-variable ODE — the standard cart-pole equations, stepped forward in fixed 0.02s increments. I wrote that integrator out longhand to convince myself there was no magic in it. "Environment" sounds like a big word for what is really just a physics function.

---

## 2. My hand-written controller

The intuition is easy: if the pole is leaning right, push right to get back under it. If it's *rotating* right, that also argues for pushing right. So:

```python
action = 1 if (theta + theta_dot) > 0 else 0
```

Over 100 episodes, this averages **493.1**, with a worst case of 320. Random actions average **22.4**. So reasoning got me from 22 to 493 in one line. I was pleased with this.

---

## 3. The result that ruined my afternoon

Then I tried a method with no reasoning in it at all. Generalize the rule to a weighted sum of all four numbers:

```python
action = 1 if (w @ obs) > 0 else 0
```

My hand-written rule is just the special case `w = [0, 0, 1, 1]`. Now draw 200 random `w` vectors from a normal distribution, score each one, and keep the best.

```
random actions            →  22.4 avg
my heuristic θ + θ̇        → 493.1 avg   (min 320 — it does still fail)
best of 200 random draws  → 500.0 avg   (min 500, max 500 — never once failed)

w ≈ [-0.17, 0.47, 1.37, 0.53]
```

200 blind guesses beat the controller I reasoned out, and nothing told the search what a pole angle even is. Worse — look at what it found. The dominant weight is `θ` (1.37) with `θ̇` (0.53) as a smaller correction. It **rediscovered my rule**, and then found the mixing ratio between the two terms that I never bothered to tune. It also noticed that cart velocity matters (0.47), which I had dismissed.

The entire algorithm is four lines:

```
w = random 4 numbers            ← guess
score = play the game with w    ← test the guess
keep w if it beat the record    ← select
repeat 200 times                ← search
```

That is conjecture and refutation with the conjectures generated by a random number generator. It has no memory, no gradient, no model. It is the least intelligent thing that could possibly work.

---

## 4. Why it works here: the policy is a knife, not a volume

This is the part that made the result stop feeling like a fluke.

The observation is a point in 4-dimensional space. The weights are another 4-vector. The set of points where `w · obs = 0` is a **hyperplane** — a flat 3-D sheet slicing through that 4-D space. Every possible configuration of the cart-pole system lands on one side of the sheet or the other, and which side you're on decides the push.

So the policy is a knife cutting state space in two, and random search is **randomly re-orienting the knife** until it finds an angle that keeps the pole up.

Two things fall out of that picture immediately, and both are real:

**There's no bias term, and there shouldn't be.** A plane defined by `w · obs = 0` always passes through the origin. The origin here is `(0, 0, 0, 0)` — cart centred, motionless, pole perfectly upright and still. That's the goal state. The decision boundary is *pinned to the thing you're trying to achieve*, which is exactly where you want it.

**Four numbers, but only three degrees of freedom.** Scale `w` by any positive constant and `w · obs` scales with it — but the *sign* doesn't change, so the policy is byte-for-byte identical. `[0,0,1,1]` and `[0,0,100,100]` are the same agent. Only the **direction** of `w` matters. The real search space isn't ℝ⁴, it's the surface of a sphere in 4-D.

And that's the answer to "why is 200 guesses enough?" You are not searching a volume. You are searching a set of directions, and a decent fraction of all directions happen to work.

---

## 5. The winner's curse — where the search lies to you

Here's the failure that turned out to be the real lesson, and it took deliberately starving the search to see it.

Every candidate `w` is scored by playing a few episodes and averaging. But CartPole starts each episode from a small random tilt. So the score you measure is an *estimate* of the policy's true value, computed from a tiny sample. Pick the maximum over 200 noisy estimates and you have systematically selected for **policies that got lucky**, not policies that are good.

On default settings CartPole is forgiving enough that this doesn't bite. Starve it — one evaluation episode per candidate, 30 trials — and it shows up immediately:

```
search score:  500.0   ← what the search believes it found
true score:    273–392 ← 100 fresh episodes, across seeds 1, 2, 7, 11
```

The search is confidently, measurably wrong about its own result. And on one seed out of those I tried, the 500 was *genuine*. Nothing visible on screen distinguishes the honest win from the lucky one — only the retest does.

This is why the script that does the search must also contain a block whose only job is to catch the search lying:

```
best_w  = argmax over candidates of (score on the selection episodes)
true    = score(best_w, on 100 episodes it was never selected on)
```

Two numbers, and the interesting one is the gap between them. Every serious RL result is a claim about the second number, produced by a process that optimizes the first.

One practical detail that matters more than it looks: every candidate must be scored on **identical seeds**. If each candidate gets fresh random starting states, an easy start makes a bad `w` look good, and the search ends up optimizing the seed lottery instead of the policy. The technique is called common random numbers, and deleting it is the fastest way to watch a converging search turn into a wandering one.

## 6. The three boxes

Reading my own 96-line script back, the useful structure was that it keeps three things in separate boxes that papers tend to smear together:

| box | the question it answers | where it lived in my file |
|---|---|---|
| **the policy** | given a state, which action? | one dot product |
| **the evaluation** | given a policy, how good is it? | `rollout` + `score` |
| **the search** | which policy do I try next? | the `for trial` loop |

Nearly every algorithm in the field — hill climbing, cross-entropy method, REINFORCE, DQN, PPO — is a **different answer to box 3**, with boxes 1 and 2 changing far less than the notation suggests. Random search's answer to box 3 is the dumbest available: *ignore everything you have learned and guess again.*

That's the baseline everything else has to beat. On CartPole, it isn't beaten.

---

## 7. So why does RL exist?

Because CartPole quietly hands you three gifts, and random search dies the moment you take any of them away.

**Gift 1 — a four-parameter policy space.** Swap those four weights for a small neural network with a hundred parameters and blind sampling collapses. The volume of the space explodes exponentially while the fraction of it that works does not. This is the pressure that produces **gradients**: instead of sampling the space, follow the slope.

**Gift 2 — reward on every single step.** CartPole tells you `+1` every timestep. Change it to `+1` only if you survive all 500 steps and 0 otherwise, and almost every candidate policy scores exactly zero. There is nothing to climb, because there is no signal to distinguish "fell over immediately" from "nearly made it." This is the pressure that produces **credit assignment and value functions** — the machinery for making a rare, delayed reward felt at the earlier decisions that led to it.

**Gift 3 — full observability.** You are handed all four state variables. Hide the two *velocities* and keep only the positions, and no memoryless policy can work, on principle — a pole at 5° falling fast and a pole at 5° recovering look identical, and require opposite actions. This is the pressure that forces you to take the **Markov property** seriously, and to build agents with memory.

Which reframes the whole field for me. The algorithms aren't a curriculum of increasingly clever tricks. Each one is the answer to a specific way that the previous, simpler method breaks:

| what breaks | what you have to invent |
|---|---|
| blind sampling in a big space | local improvement — hill climbing |
| hill climbing in a *very* big space | gradients |
| gradients with no per-step reward | credit assignment, discounted returns, value functions |
| value functions with hidden state | memory, and a real definition of "state" |

I would not have understood why policy gradients exist if I had read about them before watching search succeed and then engineering its failure myself. Meeting the answer before you've felt the problem is exactly the mode of learning I'm trying to avoid.

## 8. What I'd tell myself before starting

- **`render_mode="human"` locks the loop to 50 fps.** Training with rendering on takes approximately forever. Drop the argument entirely and you get tens of thousands of steps per second; render only to watch the winner.
- **Score on too few episodes and you will "find" great weights that collapse on retest.** That gap between estimated and true policy value isn't a beginner's mistake to be avoided — it's the central difficulty of the entire field, and it's worth reproducing on purpose.
- **Don't install a deep-learning framework yet.** Random search and hill climbing never differentiate anything; they're pure numpy. Install torch at the point where "I need gradients" is a conclusion you reached, not a step someone told you to take.
