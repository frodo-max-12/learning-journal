# Wealth as a Repertoire of Physical Transformations

**Context:** Deutsch defines wealth as "the repertoire of physical transformations one is capable of causing." I'd absorbed that as a nice line and wanted to know whether it survives being pushed on. My reading was that if wealth is transformations, then eventually the real measure isn't money or even compute — it's the set of programs or explanations you have. That turned out to be *directionally* right and imprecise in a way worth fixing, and pushing further produced the better question: **what stays scarce when transformations become cheap?**

---

## 1. What the definition actually claims

Three things to notice, none of which I'd registered on first reading.

**It's a capacity, not a stockpile.** Wealth isn't the gold in the vault; it's what you can *do* — including what you can do *with* the gold, since money is a claim on transformations other people can perform for you.

**It's grounded in physics.** Every economically meaningful thing — eating, healing, communicating, travelling — is ultimately a rearrangement of matter and energy. The space of physically possible transformations is fixed by the laws of physics. Wealth is **the subset of that space you can actually access.**

**It's counterfactual.** You're wealthy if you *could* cure your infection, whether or not you're currently sick.

That third property is what makes the definition earn its keep, because it explains something the stockpile view can't: **why we are vastly wealthier than past kings.** Not because we own more objects. Because antibiotics, refrigeration, anaesthesia, and instant global communication weren't available *at any price* — they weren't in the species' repertoire. A Roman emperor with unlimited gold could not buy the transformation that a cheap course of antibiotics performs, because no one could perform it.

Wealth as a stockpile makes that comparison incoherent. Wealth as accessible transformations makes it obvious.

---

## 2. Why knowledge is the load-bearing part

What determines which transformations are in your repertoire? Resources help, but the **binding constraint is knowledge** — specifically, good explanations.

Iron ore was useless rock until metallurgy. Uranium was a curiosity until nuclear physics. Sand became the substrate of computing only after solid-state physics. The forest is the same forest for the caveman and the engineer; what differs is the explanatory knowledge that turns it into shelter, paper, medicine, and fuel.

So the chain is: **knowledge → enables transformations → constitutes wealth.**

Which reframes "natural resources" entirely. There is no such thing as a resource independent of the knowledge that makes it useful. What we call resources are just the parts of the environment we currently know what to do with, and that set has only ever grown.

---

## 3. The correction to my phrasing

I'd said wealth would eventually *be* a set of programs or explanations. Two refinements, and the first is not pedantic:

**Wealth is still the transformations — the actual physical capacity.** Programs and explanations are what *generate and direct* the repertoire. They're the source code; wealth is the running process.

Why it matters: **two civilizations could hold the same explanations and have different wealth**, if one has the knowledge locked in texts nobody can read or apply. Knowledge that isn't instantiated in someone's actual capacity to act doesn't do the work. The distinction between having the recipe and being able to cook is real.

**And "compute" isn't wrong so much as one layer down.** In constructor theory the deep object is the **universal constructor** — a generalization of the universal computer that can perform any physically permitted transformation given the right program. Compute and construction are sister concepts: *programs run on universal computers; recipes run on universal constructors.* Knowledge is the program in both cases.

That parallel is the thing I'd keep. The same structure — a general-purpose machine plus a program, where all the specificity lives in the program — appears once as a claim about computation and once as a claim about physical capability.

---

## 4. On measurement — a deflationary answer

I wanted a metric, and there isn't one, for a defensible reason.

Every existing measure — money, GDP, compute, energy consumption — is a **proxy, and a leaky one**, because a large share of the modern wealth explosion is invisible to all of them. A free encyclopedia adds essentially nothing to GDP and adds enormously to humanity's repertoire. Any measure denominated in transactions systematically misses transformations that became free.

The honest position is that wealth is in principle unbounded, because knowledge growth is unbounded, and any single number will misrepresent it. If pushed, the closest thing to a true metric is the *size of the set of transformations our knowledge makes possible* — which isn't a scalar you can put on a price tag.

I find that unsatisfying and I think it's correct, which is a combination worth sitting with. The demand for a number is often a demand to make something comparable that isn't.

---

## 5. What gets traded when transformations are cheap?

My next question was practical: if not money, then what? The reframe I got was better than an answer.

**Trade exists wherever there's an asymmetry plus a need to coordinate.** So the right question isn't "what's the future currency?" but **"what stays scarce when transformations get cheap?"**

Run it in layers:

**The thermodynamic floor stays.** Every transformation has a free-energy cost — Landauer's principle puts a bound even on erasing a single bit. Energy is plausibly a permanent denominator, the one thing you can't argue your way out of needing.

**Constructor time is the middle layer.** Joules without a program transform nothing useful, so access to a general-purpose constructor executing specific recipes is a second axis — traded roughly the way GPU hours are traded now.

**And the deepest scarcity is novel explanations.** Here's the argument I found genuinely clarifying: **knowledge is non-rival once created.** I can give you a theorem without losing it. So *existing* knowledge doesn't sustain trade — it diffuses. What stays scarce is the **creation** of new good explanations: the unsolved problem, the next conjecture.

And creativity is unusual in being unbounded on the supply side while permanently scarce at the frontier, because every solved problem reveals deeper ones.

---

## 6. What agents compete over

I pushed on the part that seemed obviously wrong to me: post-scarcity thinking that assumes competition ends. It doesn't. **Material abundance doesn't dissolve competition, it changes the axis** — because some things are *structurally* scarce regardless of resources.

**Status and recognition** are mathematically forced to stay competitive: by definition not everyone is above the median. Wealth doesn't stop this; it relocates it to subtler axes.

**Attention** is finite at any moment. If you want anyone to engage with your work, you're in a zero-sum contest with everyone else's — and this gets *more* acute as the supply of generated content explodes, not less.

**Frontier attribution.** Everyone benefits from a discovery (positive-sum), but there's only one *first*. Science already runs on this: the knowledge is shared, the credit is rivalrous.

**Influence over which futures get built.** Once you can make anything, the question becomes *what will actually be made, toward what ends, in what order?* Being the agent whose judgment shapes the trajectory is a real and fierce competition that has nothing to do with money.

**And singular perspective.** In a world where any artifact can be copied, being a particular irreproducible viewpoint is the ultimate scarcity. Original perspectives don't scale.

What all five have in common, and what I hadn't seen: **they're features of a network of minds, not of the physical world.** They can't be manufactured with energy, because they aren't transformations at all. Which is why a definition of wealth grounded entirely in physical capacity is *incomplete* as a theory of what agents will want — and Deutsch would agree, since his claim is about wealth specifically, not about everything valuable.

The addition he'd insist on: this competition can be **positive-sum**. The race to produce the next breakthrough enriches everyone even though one agent gets the credit.

---

## 7. What I took away

**A definition earns its keep by settling comparisons the old one couldn't.** "Are we richer than a Roman emperor?" is unanswerable with stockpiles and trivial with repertoires. That's the test I'd now apply to any redefinition: what question does it make decidable?

**Non-rivalry is why knowledge behaves differently from everything else.** Give away a physical thing and you don't have it; give away an explanation and you both do. Almost everything strange about the economics of information follows from that one asymmetry, including why the frontier stays scarce while the interior becomes free.

**And the refusal to predict is a position, not an evasion.** Because knowledge growth is open-ended, the *content* of future trade is unforeseeable in principle — a medieval economist could not have priced a domain name. What's forecastable is the *form*: trade tracks whatever remains scarce after the current frontier gets absorbed. That's a genuinely different kind of claim, and I'd rather have it than a confident wrong number.
